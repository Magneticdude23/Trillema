Read before you take office
The impossible trinity: you can only ever have two of a fixed exchange rate, free capital flows, and your own interest rate. The controls below don't stop you from wanting all three — they just make the third one expensive. Watch the Monetary autonomy bar: it tells you how much of your rate you actually still control.
Policy rateInterest rate
Raise it to cool inflation and attract carry inflows; cut it to boost growth. Use it: as your main lever whenever autonomy is high. Under a peg with open capital, moving it far from the world rate drains reserves instead of working.
QE / QTBalance sheet
Buy assets (QE) to add stimulus once your policy rate is near zero; sell (QT) to drain excess liquidity. QE also compresses borrowing costs across the board — including your own government's — while QT raises them. Use it: when the rate alone isn't enough — QE adds a further growth nudge and cheapens debt service, but weakens the currency and adds to inflation.
Exchange-rate regimeFloat / Managed / Peg
Choose how hard you defend a fixed rate. Float lets the market set it; Peg locks it and spends reserves to hold it. Use it: Peg for stability if you have reserves to spare; Float when you need your interest rate free.
Defend / AccumulateFX intervention
A direct, one-off spend or purchase of reserves to push the exchange rate the way you want it right now. Use it: defend during a speculative attack; accumulate when reserves are healthy and the currency is firm.
Capital account opennessSlider
How freely money can cross your border. Closing it lets you keep both a peg and your own rate. Use it: close it if you must defend a peg while keeping a low rate — you'll pay in slower growth and lower approval, not reserves.
TariffsTrade policy
Taxes on imports. They help net exports and can prop up a weak currency's trade balance. Use it: sparingly — push too far and trading partners retaliate, dragging your own exports back down.
Fiscal stanceSlider
Government spending and taxation, independent of the central bank. Its effect on output isn't 1-for-1 — it runs through the standard open-economy multiplier, 1/(1 − c(1−τ) + m), where c is how much of extra income people spend, τ is your tax rate, and m is how much leaks abroad through imports. More open economies (see Capital account openness) have smaller multipliers, because more of the stimulus leaks out. Running a deficit also nudges up your borrowing rate immediately — a bond-market reaction — on top of the slower drag through accumulated debt. Use it: as your main stabiliser once the trinity has pinned your interest rate — it's the one lever the peg can't touch.
Tax rateSlider — automatic stabilizers
Tax revenue is (to first order) τ×GDP, so a recession shrinks revenue and widens the deficit automatically — with zero policy action from you. That's the textbook "automatic stabilizer": the budget looks worse in a downturn even if you never touch the fiscal slider, because output fell and so did tax collection. A higher tax rate means bigger automatic stabilizers (the budget cushions the cycle harder) but a smaller fiscal multiplier when you do use the slider (more of every dollar of stimulus gets taxed back before it can circulate). Watch it: the dashboard shows your current automatic-stabilizer contribution separately from your discretionary choice.
Foreign-currency borrowingOne-off
Raises reserves fast and cheap. The catch: the debt is in foreign currency, so a devaluation makes it heavier overnight. Use it: only if you're confident the peg will hold — it's a bet against your own currency falling.
Public debt / GDPAutomatic — driven by fiscal stance
Every unit of fiscal stimulus runs roughly a 1%-of-GDP deficit, which accumulates into debt. The ratio then evolves by the textbook identity: it grows when your average borrowing rate exceeds nominal GDP growth (r > g), and shrinks when growth outruns it. Debt above ~60% of GDP pushes your borrowing rate up further — a feedback loop. Watch it: past 220% you default; approval also erodes past 100%.
Peg againstSetup screen — illustrative anchor
Picks which currency's rate & inflation you're implicitly measured against (USD, EUR, JPY, GBP, CNY) — a stylised starting point, not a live feed. Use it: to flavor a scenario — e.g. peg to GBP for a Bretton-Woods-era sterling-area setup. Live cross-rates are a planned future addition.
Simulation speed0.5× – 4×
Controls how fast the ten-year run plays out. Use it: slow down around a crisis, speed up through calm years.
